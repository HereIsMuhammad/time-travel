# Gravitational Time Dilation

> "Put a clock at sea level and one on a mountain. Wait a while. They no longer agree." This is the strange, real consequence of Einstein's General Relativity (1915).

## The Thought Experiment

Imagine two identical clocks. One sits at sea level. The other sits on top of a tall mountain, far from Earth's center of mass.

After a year, you bring them back together and compare them. The mountain clock is ahead. It ticked faster.

This isn't a measurement error. Gravity itself actually slows down time, and the closer you are to a massive object, the slower your clock runs compared to someone farther away.

## The Core Idea

The stronger the gravitational field you're in, the slower time passes for you, compared to someone in a weaker field.

The formula that describes this (for a non-rotating mass):

```
t' = t × √(1 - 2GM/rc²)
```

Where:
- `t'` = time experienced by the observer near the mass (dilated time)
- `t` = time experienced by a distant observer, far from any gravity
- `G` = gravitational constant
- `M` = mass of the object
- `r` = distance from the center of the mass
- `c` = speed of light

**Key takeaway:** as `r` gets smaller (you get closer to the mass) or `M` gets bigger (the object is more massive), the term under the square root shrinks. Time slows down more and more. If `r` shrinks all the way down to `2GM/c²`, the term hits zero. That distance is called the **event horizon**, the edge of a black hole.

## How Much Does It Actually Matter?

| Location | Time dilation vs deep space |
|---|---|
| Earth's surface | slower by about 0.0000000007 (0.7 parts in a billion) |
| Sun's surface | slower by about 0.000002 |
| Neutron star surface | slower by a few percent |
| Just outside a black hole's event horizon | slower by huge, unbounded factors |

Even on Earth, the effect is tiny but very real, and we can measure it with precise enough clocks.

## Real World Proof: GPS Satellites (again!)

Remember GPS satellites from the velocity time dilation notes? Here's the other half of the story.

GPS satellites orbit about 20,000 km above Earth, much farther from Earth's mass than we are. Weaker gravity up there means their clocks run faster than clocks on the ground, by about 45 microseconds per day.

Meanwhile their high orbital speed makes their clocks run slower, by about 7 microseconds per day (velocity time dilation).

Net result: GPS clocks gain about 38 microseconds per day overall. Engineers pre-correct every satellite clock for this before launch. Without it, GPS would drift by kilometers within a single day.

## Real World Proof: Mountains and Basements

In 2010, physicists at NIST placed one atomic clock about 33 centimeters higher than another. The higher clock ran measurably faster. Even a foot of extra height changes how fast time flows, and modern atomic clocks are precise enough to catch it.

## The Path Toward Black Holes

Gravitational time dilation is the bridge between everyday physics and the extreme physics of black holes.

As you approach a very massive, very compact object:
- Time for you slows down more and more, as seen by a distant observer.
- Light climbing away from the object loses energy and shifts toward red (gravitational redshift).
- At the event horizon, the math says time dilation becomes infinite, from the outside observer's point of view.

This is exactly why black holes appear to "freeze" anything falling into them, forever, from far away.

## Mind-Bender

If you parked a spaceship just outside a black hole's event horizon for what felt like one hour to you, thousands or even millions of years could pass back on Earth, depending on how close you got. You'd return to a civilization that has aged far beyond you, or one that no longer exists.

## Key Takeaways

- Gravity doesn't just pull on objects, it slows down time itself.
- The stronger the gravity (or the more massive the object), the slower time runs nearby.
- It's been measured directly, even across a few centimeters of height difference.
- Push this effect to its extreme, near an incredibly massive, compact object, and you get a black hole.
